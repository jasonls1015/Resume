# Document Usability Research

# Presentation Type, Perceived Credibility, and Perceived Validity: A Document Usability Analysis

## Overview

For my Master's thesis in Professional and Technical Communications at NJIT, I ran an empirical study on a question I'd been asking on the job as a technical writer: does the way you format a procedure change whether readers trust it? Specifically, I tested whether presenting the same procedural content as a decision table versus a numbered list affected readers' perceived credibility and perceived validity of that content, and whether that effect held consistent across cultures.

The study used real procedures pulled from a microchip product specification, with 15 subjects from Agere Systems, stratified into Eastern and Western cultural groups based on country of origin.

## Method

Subjects read four procedures, two formatted as decision tables and two as numbered lists, drawn directly from production documentation. They did not perform the steps, only read them, then answered survey questions measuring perceived credibility (is this information accurate and trustworthy?) and perceived validity (could I actually carry this out?) on a five-point scale.

Cultural grouping was necessarily a simplification. Rather than administering a full instrument like Hofstede's Values Survey Module, subjects were grouped by country of origin as a practical surrogate for a more complex construct.

## Findings

Two-tailed t-tests found statistically significant differences between the Eastern and Western groups on all three variables tested:

- Western subjects rated tables higher than numbered lists for both perceived credibility and perceived validity (t = 2.226, p = 0.027).
- Eastern subjects rated both formats higher than Western subjects did on perceived credibility (t = 2.822, p = 0.005).
- Eastern subjects rated both formats lower than Western subjects did on perceived validity (t = -2.120, p = 0.035).

With n = 15, this was an exploratory study designed to generate testable hypotheses, not to establish generalizable findings. But the directionality was consistent and statistically significant within the sample.

## Why this matters

Twenty years into a technical writing career, I still make a version of this decision on nearly every project: table or list, structured or prose, dense or spaced out. This research is the reason I don't treat that as a purely aesthetic call. Presentation choices carry an implicit credibility signal, and that signal isn't universal. What reads as authoritative to one audience can read as unnecessarily complex, or insufficiently rigorous, to another.

The study's conclusion holds up as a practical rule I still apply: understand your reader's context before you choose a design pattern. Don't reach for a complex table when a simple list will do, and don't compress a numbered list into a dense table just because it looks more technical.
